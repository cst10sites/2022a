The Discovery of Dopamine

Dopamine as an independent neurotransmitter in the nervous system was discovered by the pharmacologist and neuroscientist Arvid Carlsson in 1957. This discovery has had a tremendous impact in the field of neuroscience, psychology, psychiatry, and neuropsychopharmacology. Arvid Carlsson’s discovery, in combination with this later work, such as his invention of the worlds first Parkinson’s disease treating drug, dihydroxyphenylalanine, the precursor ingredient for dopamine synthesis in the brain, won him the Nobel Prize for Physiology & Medicine in 2000. Dr Carlsson’s research also discovered that dopamine heavily influenced the release of all other known neurotransmitters — both today and at the time — ranging from inhibitory neurotransmitters like gamma amino butyric acid and endogenous opioids, to excitatory ones like norepinephrine and glutamate, along with the fact that dopamine can be either an excitatory or inhibitory neurotransmitter, depending on the types of dopamine being released and the type of receptors present.

The discovery of dopamine as a neurotransmitter in the nervous system forever shaped the future of neuroscience and has been extremely crucial in our understanding of how the brain works in health and disease — and so much more. Carlsson’s demonstration of dopamine having such a fundamental and critical role in almost all aspects of behaviour, from fine motor control to mood regulation, cognition, addiction, appetite, reward, sleep, learning ability, short and long-term memory, and the many physical bodily functions, such as kidney blood flow, cardiac output and contractility, digestion, and heart rate, along with the now known fact that most mental illness’ are primarily influenced by dopamine, has forever cemented dopamines’ place as being at the forefront of psychopharmacological research.
