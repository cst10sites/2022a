How Stimulants Influence Dopamine Release and Re-uptake

Psychoactive drugs are substances that temporarily alter the levels of neurotransmitters in the brain, particularly dopamine. There are six categories of psychoactive drugs, which include but are not limited to: stimulants and depressants which are the two main categories, along with opioids, cannabis, and hallucinogens, and designer drugs which are which are neither stimulants nor depressants, but can be either at select doses. The category most involved with dopamine are stimulants.

Stimulants, also referred to as psychostimulants are a class of drugs which increase the levels of excitatory neurotransmitters in the brain, most notably dopamine and norepinephrine, as well as serotonin. They are widely used throughout the world as both prescription medicines as well as without a prescription, usually for illicit purposes such as being performance-enhancers and recreational drugs.

Stimulants in medicinal doses, like those given to patients with ADHD, increase the patients ability to focus, have vigour, socialize, increase libido and elevate mood, however in high doses, stimulants can actually decrease ability to focus, which is a principle of the Yerkes-Dodson Law. High doses also produce extreme euphoria, vigour, and decrease the need for sleep.

Most, but not all stimulants have ergogenic effects. The ergogenic effects of stimulants well-documented in ones such as caffeine, ephedrine, pseudoephedrine, amphetamines and methylphenidate, with cocaine actually having the opposite effect, therefore making you physically weaker. The neurocognitive enhancing effects of stimulants, specifically amphetamine, modafinil and methylphenidate have also been well documented in healthy adolescents and young adults.

In certain cases, due to a genetic mutation, psychiatric conditions may arise such as stimulant-induced psychosis, paranoia and suicidal ideation. High doses have been reportedly associated with homicide, paranoia, aggressive and violent behaviour, motor dysfunction and motor tics. The aggressive and sometimes violent behaviour associated with high doses of stimulants is sometimes driven by paranoia or stimulant psychosis.

Almost all stimulant drugs are sympathomimetics, meaning they stimulate the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system, particularly dopamine and norepinephrine. This ultimately leads to both physical effects such as increased heart rate, increased blood pressure, respiratory rate and body temperature, as well as the many dozens of mental effects. When these changes become pathological, usually due to many months or years of use, they become permanent arrhythmia, hypertension, hyperthermia, which may further lead to rhabdomyolysis, stroke, cardiac arrest, seizures, and coma. Due to the complexity of the mechanisms that cause these potentially fatal outcomes from high to extremely high dose psychostimulants, it is almost impossible to determine a fatal dose for someone in particular.

Almost all stimulants work by enhancing phenethylamine and catecholamine neurotransmission, almost exclusively dopamine and norepinephrine. Phenethylamine and catecholamine neurotransmitters, like dopamine, are employed in regulatory pathways responsible for attention, arousal, motivation, task salience, and reward anticipation. Classical stimulants work by either blocking the re-uptake or stimulating the release of dopamine, in some cases both, which causes increased activity, due to the fact that molecularly, psychostimulants are very similar to dopamine and norepinephrine, with amphetamine and methamphetamine having a near identical molecular structure. We will look at two psychostimulants which work in differing ways, first being cocaine.

COCAINE

Cocaine, also known as methyl (1R,2R,3S,5S)-3- (benzoyloxy)-8-methyl-8-azabicyclo[3.2.1] octane-2-carboxylate, is a naturally-derived stimulant of the coca species native to South America. Cocaine works solely by blocking the re-uptake of dopamine, not by releasing it. Cocaine cannot stimulate release of dopamine as it does not fit in to the receiving dopamine receptor due to physical limitations. Cocaine works by being molecularly similar enough to dopamine to the point where it physically does not fit through the dopamine transporter, which is what is responsible for dopamine re-uptake, due to it being slightly larger than a dopamine molecule and therefore blocking dopamine from being reabsorbed. This causes those still normal amounts of dopamine being released into the receiving neuron to build up in between the transmitting and receiving neuron, causing the neuron to go into a temporary overdrive, with a peak at around 10 minutes post ingestion. Cocaine increases dopamine levels by approximately 450%. Eventually, specialized enzymes in the brain designed to break down and remove foreign substances wherever in the neurons begin breaking down the substance and sending it to the liver for further processing and ultimately the kidneys for excretion. Cocaine is broken down fairly quickly, with an elimination half-life of 60 minutes, meaning that after one hour, half of the drug is removed from the body. Cocaine does not inhibit the brains specialized enzymes from breaking it down, which other stimulants do inhibit.

AMPHETAMINEs

Amphetamine and methamphetamine, also known as alpha-methylphenethylamine and methyl-alpha-methylphenethylamine respectively, differ from cocaine and many other stimulants like caffeine due to the fact that they are also both phenethylamines and catecholamines, and nearly identical in molecular structure to dopamine, being only minuscule amounts larger. Amphetamine’s full name is alpha-methyl phenethylamine, with methamphetamine being methyl-alpha-methylphenethylamine. Amphetamines, due to the fact that they are nearly identical molecularly to dopamine, amphetamines being only slightly larger, are highly potent stimulants. They work by not only both blocking the reuptake, stimulating the release of dopamine, but also inhibiting certain types of brain enzymes, including but not limited to monoamine oxidase.

Amphetamines do this by entering the dopamine receptor (receiving neuron) and due to it being a slightly larger molecule, yet not large enough not to fit into the reflector, it fits in to the dopamine receptor more snug and actually ends up causing more stimulation than a real dopamine molecule could because it’s covering more surface area of the receptor and is therefore harder to be broken down and removed; secondly, amphetamines block the reabsorption of dopamine via the dopamine transporter by blocking it in a similar fashion to cocaine, being that it blocks it to a point where the amphetamine molecule is essentially physically stuck in the dopamine transporter; and thirdly amphetamines also block release of certain brain enzymes, including but not limited to monoamine oxidase, which break down foreign substances in the brain. Due to amphetamines being remarkably lipophilic, they are difficult to remove from the brain and body, and remain unchanged in the brain for long periods of time. After 24 hours, on average only 64% of either amphetamine or methamphetamine remains completely unchanged in the body and brain, due to not only the fact that these are difficult substances for the body to break down because of how lipophilic they are causing the molecules remain physically stuck in the body for long periods of time, and also because amphetamine is first broken down by the liver into 4-hydroxyamphetamine, which is still psychoactive; methamphetamine is first broken down into amphetamine, and then 4-hydroxyamphetamine. 4-hydroxyamphetamine is further broken down into 4-hydroxynorephedrine, then 4-hydroxyphenylacetone, then benzoic acid, then hippuric acid, norephedrine, and lastly phenylacetone, which is then broken down further into simple elements, though the body removes most of the amphetamines through the kidneys instead of the liver due to the complexity of breaking down these molecules. Amphetamine and methamphetamine have a half life ranging from 5-30 hours depending on biological factors. Amphetamines, due to their dual-functionality in neurons and their extremely potent effects, can, at high doses, cause so many dopamine receptors to be occupied by amphetamine molecule, plus re-uptake being blocked and the inhibition of the enzymes, that you actually cannot go any higher in terms of the amount of dopamine stimulation, due to their being a limit of dopamine receptors in the brain, which is for a reason. Studies have indicated that amphetamines at high doses raised dopamine levels by up to 1,250%, which is the theoretical limit for how much dopamine the brain has the capacity to feel.

Due to the combination of the three methods amphetamines use to work so effectively the brain compared to other stimulants, the brain diverges to another form of self-protection which is via cellular shut down of over-excited receptors, when the enzymes for breaking down foreign substances are either inhibited or not up to the job. These shut down receptors will eventually be returned to normality once homeostasis has returned. This is also why amphetamines have a significant crash, regarded as one of the worst among drugs, sometimes so bad that in extreme cases even first time users of methamphetamine have ended up committing suicide during the crash. This is due to the fact that these stimulants are so powerful that they cause the brain to actually overdo it’s secondary self-protection attempt being the neuronal shutdown in response to the drug. Some also argue that this over the top shutdown of things in response to drugs not only limited to stimulants is to actually discourage you from engaging further with the substance.
